# Global Map of Commercial Shipping Traffic (`GlobalShippingImpactsHalpernetal.jpg`)

## Overview
A full-resolution global map in a Mollweide-style pseudocylindrical projection illustrating the spatial distribution and intensity of commercial shipping traffic across the world's oceans, from the 2008 *Science* study "A Global Map of Human Impact on Marine Ecosystems" by Halpern et al.

## Map & Data Details
- **Continents**: All landmasses—including North and South America, Europe, Africa, Asia, Australia, and Antarctica—are rendered in uniform flat dark gray against a white background outside the globe boundary.
- **Color Scale & Intensity**: Ocean areas are color-coded by shipping track density and impact intensity, ranging from deep blue (lowest traffic) through cyan/light blue, yellow, and orange to bright red (highest concentration of vessel traffic):
  - **High-Intensity Corridors (Red/Orange)**: Dense red bands highlight major global shipping lanes, including the North Atlantic routes between the U.S. East Coast/Gulf of Mexico and Western Europe, the English Channel and North Sea, the Mediterranean Sea through the Suez Canal and Red Sea, the Indian Ocean trunk route past Sri Lanka through the Strait of Malacca, the South and East China Seas up to Japan, and North Pacific great-circle routes connecting East Asia with the U.S. West Coast and Panama Canal. Distinct red-orange tracks also follow the coasts of South America and West/South Africa around the Cape of Good Hope.
  - **Moderate Traffic Zones (Yellow/Cyan)**: Broad yellow and light-blue swaths fill the northern subtropics and mid-latitudes of the North Atlantic and North Pacific, as well as the northern Indian Ocean and crisscrossing diagonal routes between major southern hemisphere ports (such as Cape Town, Buenos Aires, Perth, and New Zealand).
  - **Low Traffic & Polar Regions (Dark Blue/White)**: The South Pacific, southern Indian Ocean, and high-latitude Arctic and Southern Oceans show sparse individual blue tracklines with uncolored white gaps where no ship tracks were recorded, particularly across the southern high latitudes near Antarctica.
