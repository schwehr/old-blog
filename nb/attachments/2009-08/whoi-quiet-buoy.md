# WHOI Auto-Detection Quiet Buoy Diagram (`whoi-quiet-buoy.png`)

## Overview
An illustrated two-panel scientific diagram showing the design and mooring components of a Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI) acoustic auto-detection buoy ("quiet buoy") used for monitoring marine mammals such as North Atlantic right whales. The left panel depicts the buoy mooring in calm seas, while the right panel demonstrates how the stretchable "Gumby hose" decouples surface wave motion in rough seas to keep the underwater hydrophone quiet.

## Diagram Panels & Components
- **Left Panel (Calm Seas):**
  - Shows a calm ocean surface under a clear gradient sky.
  - A yellow and blue surface buoy marked `WHOI` floats upright at the surface, labeled **Auto-detection buoy**.
  - Extending vertically downward from the base of the buoy is a black flexible tether labeled **Gumby hose**.
  - Below the hose is a round yellow subsurface float labeled **Steel flotation sphere**.
  - Suspended on a vertical line beneath the sphere is a yellow cylindrical acoustic sensor labeled **Hydrophone**. To the right of the hydrophone, the head and pectoral fin of a North Atlantic right whale (with characteristic callosities on its head) are visible in the dark mid-water.
  - Near the rocky seafloor, the mooring line connects to a silver and yellow cylindrical **Mooring release** mechanism, which is chained to a flat circular **Anchor** resting on the seabed.
- **Right Panel (Rough Seas):**
  - Depicts stormy weather with dark clouds, diagonal rain streaks, and large, foaming surface waves.
  - The yellow and blue `WHOI` surface buoy is pitched at a steep angle on the crest of a wave.
  - The black Gumby hose stretches and curves diagonally through the upper water column, absorbing the heave and surge of the surface buoy.
  - Despite the rough surface conditions, the yellow **Steel flotation sphere**, **Hydrophone**, **Mooring release**, and **Anchor** remain stationary and vertically aligned in the calm deeper water below, while a full right whale swims past the hydrophone.
