AUTHOR: Kurt

BODY:
We just had a Mac where the disk was suddenly full.  I never really
figured out what was chewing up all the disk space, but I learned some
really useful Mac specific tricks in the process after spending a
while with du, df, lsof, find, and ls -a, and xargs.  With some help
from the fink community (thanks Jack!), I tried out <a
href="http://www.derlien.com/">Disk Inventory X</a>.  It takes a while
to plow through your disk, but then is super easy to see what's going on
space wise right now.

<img title="Disk Inventory X" withgrayborder="True"
src="http://schwehr.org/blog/attachments/2010-04/diskinventoryx.png"/>

Then, I had the thought to use <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_Machine_%28Apple_software%29">Time Machine</a> to watch the differences
between the backups.  That should definitely record what just started
eating up disk space (assuming it wasn't in part of a disk that was
marked to not be backed up).  TimeTracker (at <a href="http://charlessoft.com/">charlessoft.com</a> does a great job of giving a quick view into what has changed.

<img title="TimeTracker to look into Time Machine backups" withgrayborder="True"
src="http://schwehr.org/blog/attachments/2010-04/timetracker.png"/>

I usually prefer command line ways to work.  While <a
href="http://www.fernlightning.com/doku.php?id=software:misc:tms">tms</a>
(described <a
href="http://www.macgurulounge.com/see-which-files-time-machine-backed-up/">here</a>)
looks like an awesome command line utility, the software is closed and
appears to be dead.  However, in the future, it looks like <a
href="http://code.google.com/p/timedog/">timedog</a> might be really
useful.  And timedog is open source perl, so it won't just up and
disappear.

See also: <a href="http://www.macosxhints.com/article.php?story=20071109024812437">10.5: Compare Time Machine backups to original drive System 10.5</a> [macosxhints] and <a href="http://forums.macosxhints.com/showthread.php?p=579121">my question on the forums</a>
