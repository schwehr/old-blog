On Tuesday Dec 14th, the USCG switched my Class A Blue Force CNS
6000 AIS transceiver and the NOAA SBNMS Shine Micro receiver (joined
by a splitter) to a desktop L3 ATON AIS unit.  I would have liked to
have kept the Shine Micro receiver as the device feeding NAIS, but the
USCG recomended going with just the L3 ATON for both receive and
transmit.  Thanks to <a href="http://www.ifaw.org/">IFAW</a> for funding the purchase of the ATON unit.

I was not able to go along as I was sitting on a plane at the time, so
these photos are courtesy of Mike Thompson.  The yellow box is the
ATON.  This makes the system much less complicated.  The CNS 6000 has
three separate boxes: the grey unit with the RF hardware and
controller, a black LCD display and keyboard (the required Minimum
Keyboard Display - MKD), and a junction box.

This is the basement of the visitor center at the Cape Cod National
Seashores next to Province Town, MA.  In the first image, the
controller computer is also using a web browser to display AIS traffic
in the area.  I believe that is EarthNC Online.

General debugging tips: If you are getting VSWR errors from an AIS
device, first check that all of your connectors are firmly seated and
screwed down hand tight.  If you still have VSWR errors, the next
thing to do is check for corrosion in the connectors.  Give them a
good cleaning.  If you really have a mismatched antenna, the best
thing to do is to purchase the correct antenna.  Shine Micro has lots
of high quality, high gain antennas.  If that is not an option
(e.g. you can't change the antenna), consider purchasing an <a
href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_tuner">antenna tuner</a>.
You will loose some energy in the tuner, but you will then be able to
use the system once it is impediance matched.  Consult your local
electrical engineer or amateur radio operator if need help.

It is not a good idea to open up the limits for VSWR in the firmware
of your AIS device.  The <a
href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_wave_ratio">Voltage
Standing Wave Ratio (VSWR)</a> is a measure of how much energy is
being reflected back to the transmitter and not going out the antenna.
A 1:1 VSWR means you have a perfect antenna.  Larger VSWRs mean you
are sending less energy out of the antenna.

I suggest purchasing the <a
href="http://www.arrl.org/shop/The-ARRL-Handbook-2011-Hardcover-Edition/">The
ARRL Handbook for Radio Communications</a>.  There is a wikibook, <a
href="http://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Amateur_Radio_Manual">Amateur Radio
Manual</a>, but it not very far along.
